Manage care successfully

Boundaries, burnout & ending well

Warmth and clear limits aren't opposites, the best caregiving relationships have both. Here's how to keep things healthy, prevent burnout, and part ways gracefully if you need to.

Boundaries make it last

Being kind to a caregiver doesn't mean having no limits, in fact, clear expectations are a kindness. Everyone does better when the hours, the role, the pay, and how you communicate are spelled out. A work agreement does most of this for you. Revisit it if things drift.

Preventing burnout, theirs and yours

Caregiving is heavy work. A burned-out caregiver can't give good care, and a burned-out family caregiver is just as real a risk.

  • Build in real breaks and days off, and honor them.
  • Keep a backup plan so no one is trapped by a single point of failure.
  • Consider respite care so family caregivers can rest too.
  • Notice the warning signs, exhaustion, resentment, short temper, and respond before it's a crisis.
If you're the one running on empty: that's not weakness, it's a load injury, and it's common. Ask for specific help, and if you ever feel hopeless or overwhelmed, reach out, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is there 24/7. Our Dementia Journey course has a whole module on caregiver burnout and accepting help.

When it's time to end employment

Sometimes, even with good intentions, it isn't working. You can end the arrangement, and you can do it with dignity:

  • Be honest, clear, and kind. You don't have to over-explain.
  • Give reasonable notice when you safely can, or pay in lieu of notice.
  • Handle the practical close-out: final pay, and the return of keys, codes, and any property.
  • Line up replacement care first if possible, so there's no gap.
  • Thank them for what they did well. People remember how they were let go.

Never be left without coverage

Keeping a backup caregiver on your bench protects your loved one, and lets everyone take a real break.
