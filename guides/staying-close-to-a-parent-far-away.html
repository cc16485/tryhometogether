Staying close

How to stay close to a parent who lives far away

Loving a parent from a few states away comes with a particular ache: the worry between visits, the guilt, the sense that a phone call isn't quite enough. You can't move closer overnight, but you can close the distance in the ways that matter most.

Long-distance caregiving is more common than ever. Families spread out for work and life, and then a parent gets older, and suddenly the miles feel very heavy. The good news is that "close" was never really about geography. It's about presence, rhythm, and knowing how they're truly doing. All three are within reach from anywhere.

Build a rhythm they can count on

Scattered, unpredictable contact leaves everyone anxious. A steady rhythm does the opposite. It gives your parent something to look forward to and gives you a natural moment to notice if anything seems off.

  • A short daily face-to-face, even two minutes at the same time each day, so the days have shape.
  • A longer weekly catch-up, maybe Sunday, where the whole family can drop in.
  • Shared small moments, a grandchild's recital, opening a birthday card together, so it isn't only "checking on you."
Why seeing beats hearing: on the phone, "I'm fine" is easy to say and impossible to check. On video, you can see if they've eaten, whether they seem steady, if the home looks cared for. Your eyes tell you what their words won't.

Put a few things in place

1

Line up one local set of hands

A neighbor, a nearby relative, or a hired caregiver who can physically stop by. You cannot be the person two minutes down the road, so make sure someone is. If there's no one, this is exactly what HomeTogether Hire helps families arrange.

2

Get organized once

Keep medications, doctors, insurance, and emergency contacts in one shared place. When something happens at a distance, having the information ready turns panic into action.

3

Make seeing them effortless on their end

The best system is the one that actually gets used. If staying in touch depends on your parent managing a phone or tablet, it will happen less and less. Choose the path of least resistance for them.

Common questions

How do I care for an aging parent who lives far away?

Build a reliable rhythm of contact, put one local person on your team, keep key information organized, and use technology that lets you see them, not just hear them.

How often should I check in?

A predictable rhythm matters more than frequency. Many families do a short daily face-to-face plus a longer weekly catch-up, so your parent can count on it and you'd notice quickly if something felt off.

What's the best way to check on a parent remotely?

A quick video call, because you can see how they look and how the home seems. A TV-based device like HomeTogether makes it effortless on their end, and scheduled virtual visits add a trained set of eyes.
